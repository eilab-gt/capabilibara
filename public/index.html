Capability Provenance in Language Models

A Case Study in Social Reasoning

Where does a model learn a skill? We trace an answer back through training data, then turn millions of noisy signals into a map people can inspect.

The idea

A model answer has a training history.

A language model learns from an enormous collection of documents. When it answers a question, some of those documents pushed its training in a helpful direction; others pushed in a different direction.

Training-data attribution estimates those pushes. We use it as a discovery tool: not to declare one document the source of a capability, but to find repeated patterns across a corpus.

Dialogue “Why did she say that?” perspective and intent
Advice “Try seeing it from his side.” social response
Reference “The system has three states.” different task signal
→ → →
Model answer She was trying to understand his intent.

Influence is a signed estimate of alignment between a training document and a model response. It is evidence of association, not proof that one document created the behavior.

How it works

From millions of pushes to a map humans can read.

Individual document scores are noisy. The useful signal appears when we organize many documents, compare capabilities, and test the strongest patterns with an intervention.

  1. 1
    TraceEstimate which training documents align with a benchmark response.
  2. 2
    GroupAggregate noisy scores into labeled regions of the corpus.
  3. 3
    TestForget flagged material and measure what changes.

Walk through the full pipeline. The implementation uses TrackStar via Bergson for attribution and NGDiff for the unlearning check.

Make the noise legible

Group similar parts of the corpus before interpreting them.

We label documents by topic and by form—for example, literature written as questions and answers, or politics written as documentation. This creates repeated units we can compare instead of cherry-picking individual documents.

Compare like with like

Separate what a task is about from what it asks the model to do.

Four multiple-choice benchmarks cross domain (social or STEM) with capability type (reasoning or knowledge). That lets us ask whether a difference follows the subject matter, the kind of thinking, or both.

All four use the same evaluation style, keeping the comparison focused on capability rather than answer format.

What we learned

Capabilities leave different fingerprints in training data.

The clearest result is not a single magic bucket. It is a difference in the shape and language of the corpus regions each capability uses.

01

The shape of support changes with the capability.

In OLMo3, social reasoning draws support from a broader mixture of interpersonal and expository text. The social-knowledge and STEM tasks concentrate more strongly in documentation-like regions.

See the evidence

SocialIQA's 576-region profile correlates weakly with each comparison benchmark (r = 0.06–0.22), while the other three correlate at r = 0.76–0.86.

02

The underlying language matters more than a label alone.

High-influence social-reasoning regions split between short, dialogue-rich text about people and intent, and long-form documentation that supplies broader world structure. Social reasoning is not coming from one genre.

See an illustrative region

Literature × Customer Support is the strongest positive SocialIQA region in this OLMo3 study and points in the opposite direction for the comparison tasks. It is an informative example, not a universal category claim.

“Suppressive” means gradients oppose the benchmark query gradients under this metric; it does not mean the text is inherently harmful.
03

An intervention checks whether the map found something load-bearing.

We asked the model to forget influence-ranked training material, then compared the damage with forgetting random material from the same topics. The clearest selective effect appears for social reasoning; the other benchmarks are weaker, null, or reversed.

See the evidence

For SocialIQA, influence-targeted forgetting causes a median +1.60 percentage points more damage than the matched random control (95% CI [+1.3, +2.2], adjusted p ≈ 10−5).

This is partial causal validation of an aggregate pattern. It does not identify individually causal documents or the internal mechanism.

Limits

What this study does and does not claim.

  • Attribution is an analytic lens, not an exact proof of causal necessity for individual documents; unlearning validates aggregate patterns without eliminating approximation error.
  • Analysis runs on a 5.68M-document stratified working set drawn from the ~1.26B-document population; results should not be read as characterizing every document in the full corpus.
  • Unlearning shows a corpus region materially supports the aligned benchmark; it does not explain the mechanism by which those documents shape model behavior.
  • The full deep-dive is measured on one open-data ecosystem (OLMo3-7B / Dolma3). The Comma 7B and DCLM 7B extensions are in progress; until they land, generalization across model families remains an open question.

The contrastive design probes the structure of capability provenance. It does not evaluate, or make claims about, the social reasoning abilities of any population or demographic group.
